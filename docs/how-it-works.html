0. Launch: what to share, and when

Share one link now: the survey. Hold the landing page back for now. The survey shows the pitch only at the very end so that seeing the product can’t colour anyone’s answers. If people read the landing page first, they arrive at the survey already sold, and their answers on awareness, cost and interest stop being honest. The survey already collects launch sign-ups at its end (source “survey”), so you lose nothing by waiting.

1. The whole system on one page

Two web pages collect data. Two places store it. Nothing runs on a server of your own: the pages are plain files on GitHub Pages, and they talk directly to Supabase and to a small script inside your Google Sheet.

3. The survey: what students go through

Four sections. The header shows the section name and “2/4”, never a question count, because a visible “7 / 35” makes people quit early.

Every question is a ticket. A slim strip on top shows only the route IITR ✈ ABRD; the ✈ and a solid line move along it as they progress. A perforated tear line separates it from the question. Nothing else sits on the ticket, on purpose: the section name is already in the header, and extra labels made each screen feel crowded. The welcome screen keeps just a title, one line, an optional name field and Start; the two reasons to answer sit under a “Why answer?” dropdown.

Test mode: open the survey with ?test on the end (e.g. …/Survey_tool/?test). A yellow bar says “Test mode: nothing you answer is saved”, and nothing reaches Supabase or the Sheet. Use this whenever you or a teammate want to try it.

SectionWhoWhat it asks
1 · Getting startedEveryoneDid they know exchange exists, cost guess (starts blank so nothing anchors it), how sure, programme, year, department, optional gender and family money
2 · What you knowEveryone (+1 PG-only question)Name a partner university, who handles it, where they look for info, CGPA cutoff, when applications open, do credits count, then “Which is closest to you?”
3 · Your storyOne of three groupsA never applied: what holds them back. B started, didn’t finish: where it broke. C went: real cost, funding, course mapping, contact for juniors.
4 · Wrapping upEveryoneHow they hear about opportunities, “I’d apply if…”, follow-up permission, the pitch (“Here’s what we’re building” → “Tell me when it launches” / “Not now”), then email (required)

How the group is chosen: the answer to “Which is closest to you?” decides it. “Never seriously considered” or “thought about it, never started” → A. “Started”, “applied and wasn’t selected”, “selected but didn’t go” → B. “Been on exchange / on one now” → C.

Length by path (counted from the code): A has 35 screens with 2 required written answers; B has 38 with 2; C has 40 with 4. C is longest on purpose: returnees are the rarest and most valuable people to hear from.

The ending: when they submit the email, the last ticket flies up and away while a plane crosses the screen. Then their boarding pass flies in and lands. Reloading a finished survey shows the pass without the animation, and phones set to reduce motion skip it.

Pass fieldWhere it comes from
RouteIITR → ABRD. For returnees (group C), the first letters of where they went, e.g. MUNI
NameThe name they typed on the check-in ticket (or Guest), shown large
Seat, barcodeMade from their session ID, so every pass is unique
GateInitials of their department, e.g. PIE
StatusA: Standby · B: Rebooking · C: Landed
Priority badgeShown in the top bar only if they joined the launch list

Below the pass: “You’re checked in, Name.”, one line (“We’ll email you what we find.”), a “You’re on the launch list” tag if they said yes, and two buttons. Share my boarding pass makes a picture of the pass and opens the phone’s share sheet (or downloads it). The picture library loads only on that tap, so the survey stays fast. Send the survey to a friend shares the link instead.

Error messages: only one shows at a time, above the button. Tapping again refreshes it rather than stacking copies.

4. What happens on every tap

  1. The answer is stored in the phone’s browser first. If they close the tab and reopen the link, they continue where they left off.
  2. It joins a send queue (also kept in the browser).
  3. Each queued item goes to Supabase and the Sheet at the same time. It leaves the queue as soon as either one accepts it.
  4. If both fail (no internet), it stays queued and retries every 15 seconds and whenever the phone comes back online.
  5. If only one fails, it isn’t retried. The other copy has it. This is why Supabase has none of the October responses.
Fixed on 26 Nov: answers given while an earlier answer was still sending used to be silently dropped. It cost the “completed” marker, the email and the waitlist sign-up in the first test. The queue now re-reads itself before saving, and sends anything that arrived meanwhile. I tested 13 answers fired at once: all arrived in both places.

5. The landing page and waitlist

Flow: headline → one-line explanation → email + “Join the waitlist ↗” → secondary “Take the short survey” → three numbers → the problem → what they’ll get → a look inside (product mock-ups) → a final dark “Be the first to know” sign-up.

A look inside: a minimal dashboard in a browser window: an icon sidebar, the “Find your seat” list (university, fit bar, cost, status dot) and a real-cost panel drawn as one stacked bar with a legend. Below it, three phone screens: courses approved before (✓ badges), seniors to ask, and a countdown ring to the next deadline. On phones the dashboard becomes one column and the phone screens swipe sideways. It’s all plain HTML and CSS, no images, so it loads instantly.

  • On submit: the email is checked, then sent to Supabase (join_waitlist, source = landing) and the Sheet at the same time. Success if either accepts it.
  • Signing up twice from the same place just updates the same row. One email can appear once as “survey” and once as “landing”; that’s intended.
  • After joining: the form becomes “You’re on the list”, with buttons to take the survey or share. The browser remembers it, so a revisit shows the same state.
  • The counter (“N IITR students are already waiting”) stays hidden until there are 25 sign-ups, so it never shows a weak number.
  • The mock-ups are labelled “Example data” in the window bar, and use placeholder names (University A–D, made-up seniors) on purpose: students must not mistake them for real seats or real people.

Check before sharing: the numbers 294 MoUs, 61 countries and 24 credits come from your Notion problem statement. Confirm them against IITR’s current MoU list and Senate policy, since the page cites those as the source.

Why two waitlist counts

Survey sign-ups come from people who just spent several minutes on the topic, so they overstate demand. Landing sign-ups come from people who saw a link in a group chat, which is the honest demand signal. The Summary shows them side by side, never added together.

7. Supabase: the database, from zero

A database is a set of strict tables living on a server. Supabase hosts yours and gives it a web address. The pages use the public key, which, thanks to security rules (Row Level Security), can only call four save functions and read totals. Only you, logged in to the dashboard, can read actual rows.

Tables

answersanswersession_id, question_id (e.g. q10_cost_guess), value (text, list, or {"value":450000,"no_idea":false})

Why one row per answer: an answer is saved the instant it’s given, so a half-finished response still counts. A new question needs no database change.

Functions (the only way in for the public pages)

save_session, save_answer, save_contact, join_waitlist. Each checks and trims its input, then adds or updates a row.

-- Started vs finished

9. Verify everything yourself

About 15 minutes. Use a private window on your phone so it behaves like a fresh student.

  • Open the survey with ?from=me-test, type the name TEST-ME, answer 3 questions.Expect: a new row at the bottom of All responses within a minute, with “me-test” under Came from.
  • Close the tab, reopen the same link.Expect: you continue at question 4, with a “Welcome back” note.
  • On the cost question, look before touching anything.Expect: “₹ ?”, an empty amount box, and Continue greyed out until you set an amount or tap “I have no idea”.
  • Finish the survey, say “Tell me when it launches”, and enter an email you control.Expect: the “Thanks” screen with “You’re on the launch list”; a row in Waitlist (source survey) and in Contacts.
  • In Supabase SQL Editor run select * from survey_progress;Expect: started and completed both went up by 1.
  • Open join.html?from=me-test, enter a bad email, then a good one.Expect: “Please enter a valid email.”, then “You’re on the list.” and a Waitlist row with source landing.
  • Paste your test Response ID into Excluded, then Survey → Refresh summary.Expect: your test disappears from every count, including the survey waitlist count.
  • Clean up: delete your landing row from the Waitlist tab (it has no Response ID to exclude), and run the delete query from section 7 in Supabase.Expect: Supabase back to its previous counts.
  • Open the Sheet link in a browser where you’re not signed in.Expect: “You need access”.

10. Daily routine while collecting (5 minutes)

  1. Summary → Overview: started, finished, finish rate. Below about 50% means people are dropping out, so look at where Status stops in All responses.
  2. Responses per day and Came from: which channel delivered. Push the ones that work.
  3. Year and branch spread: if one group dominates, target the others.
  4. Skim new rows: add junk IDs to Excluded.
  5. Launch waitlist: watch the landing count, your honest demand signal.
  6. Once a week, add what you learn from IR and returnees to Typed numbers.

12. Changing things safely

  • Question wording: edit the title in index.html (search for the question’s id), then push. Safe.
  • Never change an id on a live survey: it’s the column key in both Supabase and the Sheet.
  • Don’t reword these answer options: the code or the Sheet matches them exactly.
    • “Which is closest to you?” (picks the group)
    • “If that was gone, would you apply?” (shows a follow-up)
    • CGPA “Yes, and I know roughly what it is”
    • “No, this is news to me” and “I’d heard…” (findings table)
    • The contact and permission answers
    • “Yes, tell me when it launches”
  • Adding a question: add it to the right list in index.html. To give it a nice Sheet heading, also add it to QUESTIONS in setup/3-google-sheet-script.gs, paste that into Apps Script, deploy a new version, then Survey → Rebuild.
  • Going live: commit and push to GitHub. Pages updates in about a minute.

13. Why it looks and reads this way

Shorter beats prettier. In the research, length and mobile optimisation are the strongest drivers of drop-out. Written questions were trimmed and two became optional (family conversation, “describe an exchange student”).
Tap over type. Open text questions hurt mobile completion most, so most screens are taps. Written answers have a mic button.
Thumb zone. Answers are full-width rows at least 54px tall, above the roughly 1 cm minimum tap size. The main button is pinned to the bottom of the screen.
No question counter. Section names and a thin progress line. The research shows progress bars barely change completion, and jumpy ones hurt.
Plain voice. Short, direct “you” questions and contractions, with subtitles only where they add information.
No anchoring. The cost guess starts blank; the old slider started at ₹10 lakh and nudged every guess.
The pitch comes last. Seeing the product earlier would colour the research answers.
One visual system. Plnty’s hierarchy: neutral background, near-black Geist type, black pill buttons, one red accent. Factory’s small dotted labels and plain numbers.
The ticket makes it a game. A plain Google Form loses people; a ticket that moves towards “abroad”, plus a personal boarding pass at the end, gives a reason to finish and something worth sharing.

Sources: Couper & Mavletova, meta-analysis of mobile web survey breakoffs (doi.org/10.5334/bar.f); meta-analysis of progress indicators (core.ac.uk/download/42630452.pdf); Pew Research Center, tips for mobile web surveys (2015); Nielsen Norman Group, touch target size; web.dev, form design basics; SurveyMonkey and Typeform survey-writing guidance (vendor sources, used for wording only).
